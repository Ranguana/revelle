# Revelle Société — proposed menus

A proposal, not the catalogue. Nothing here is seeded. Numbering continues from
37, after menu 36 in docs/menus.md. Format and fields match the authored file
exactly: **dishes in order · what it's for · season · how much making**, with
actually made, half made and bought and arranged as the only three values on
the making axis.

The brief these were written to: within a destination the menus have to differ
on the axes the engine selects by — occasion, season, and how much making —
so that a bigger pool is a wider pool. The gaps closed are shown in the
coverage tables below, and the research each destination drew on is cited at
the end of its section.

Existing menus are not repeated or rewritten here. Where the research suggested
one of them is off, it is listed under Observations at the end.

Drawn on: bouillabaisse in two acts, which is live practice and not folklore —
Mère Germaine in Villefranche still serves it at two prices, one for one and one
for two served in two dishes; Tetou at Golfe-Juan, founded 1917 by a navy cook
turned fisherman, is the Cannes-side house for it. Escoffier's own bouillabaisse
has no potatoes and no rouille; the broth-over-bread-first ritual predates the
1980 charter, which a 1962 table would not have heard of. The cold buffet is the
grand-hotel garde-manger table: salmon glazed in aspic, chaud-froid of chicken
decorated with truffle cut-outs, galantine pressed into a cylinder, everything
under glaze — and peach Melba, which is Escoffier's own, poached peaches on
vanilla ice cream under raspberry purée and nothing else. Melon opens two of
Escoffier's surviving gala menus. Note for the period: in 1962 grand-hotel
dining rooms were not Michelin destinations — the first chef starred inside a
palace hotel is 1980 — and the prestige signals are ceremony, French names and
tableside work rather than the producer's name. Also: Côtes de Provence was VDQS
in 1962, not AOC; the correct 1962 bottles are Tavel, Cassis, Bandol and Bellet.

Houses that belong to the period: the Carlton (1911), the Majestic under
François André, who died in Cannes on 2 May 1962 five days before the festival
opened, and the Hôtel du Cap with the Eden Roc pavilion and the pool blasted
into the rock. La Colombe d'Or at Saint-Paul, where the artists paid in
paintings. The 15th festival ran 7–23 May 1962 at the Palais Croisette. Le
Moulin de Mougins (1969) and Le Chantecler as a destination (1978) are both too
late.

Drawn on: the Borscht Belt dining rooms — Grossinger's seated 1,300, the
Concord 3,000 — and a surviving Kutsher's menu with eleven main courses
including boiled flanken, potted brisket and stuffed fresh-water fish with red
beet horseradish. The kashrut constraint is load-bearing and under-used: a meat
dinner carries no butter, no cream sauce and no cream dessert, so the dairy

1954 as the first kosher rye baked under rabbinical supervision by a major
company. S'mores as "Some-mores" first appear in a 1925 Connecticut newspaper
account of Girl Scouts. Late August in the Catskills is peaches and tomatoes at
peak, the tail of the blackberries, sweet corn, and only the first apples.

Drawn on: ma'a Tahiti, the Sunday family lunch cooked in the ahima'a, the earth
oven — a pit lined with basalt heated over coconut husks, food wrapped in banana
leaves, sealed with earth for hours: suckling pig, uru (breadfruit), taro, fafa
(chicken with taro leaves and coconut milk), po'e (fruit and starch baked in
leaves, then drowned in fresh coconut milk poured at the table) and pain coco,
which is made with coconut water rather than milk. Firi firi is explicitly a
breakfast doughnut, eaten warm, and café-pain-beurre is so embedded that it is
often the evening meal as well as the morning one. The roulottes at Place
Vai'ete in Papeete set up nightly at dusk and are Chinese-Tahitian as much as
French — the casse-croûte is a split baguette filled with chow mein or fried
potatoes, and ice cream is served in one too. Ma'a tinito, literally "Chinese
food," is pork with red beans, cabbage and macaroni, and exists nowhere in
China: it came with the Cantonese and Hakka labourers recruited in the 1860s for
the Atimaono cotton plantation, whose descendants are now about a tenth of the
population. Vanilla from Taha'a is the fine-dining signature — Tahitian vanilla
is the only kind whose pod does not split before maturity, so it is picked ripe
and stays fleshy, and half a pod does the work of a whole Bourbon one. Marché de
Papeete, open from 4:30 on Sunday mornings since the 1840s. The seasons here are
wet (November to April) and dry (May to October), not warm and cold, so the
season field is doing little work and "year-round" is the honest answer. Beef is
imported from New Zealand; a menu leaning on it would be fibbing.

Drawn on: frituras de malanga appear on La Guarida's, Doña Eutimia's and El
Cocinero's menus at the same time — they are the default Cuban starter — and
croquetas de jamón are described in Cuba's own encyclopaedia as the best-known
cold dish in the country, with pan con croqueta as the everyday street version.
Cascos de guayaba con queso, known as timba, is the standard dessert. Doña
Eutimia off the Plaza de la Catedral for ropa vieja; El Aljibe in Miramar for
the chicken in sour orange, served with frijoles negros dormidos, the "sleeping"
black beans cooked and rested so they thicken; Los Nardos opposite the Capitolio
for the old Spanish register; La Guarida, which began as twelve chairs in 1996.
Ajiaco is the big pork-and-viandas stew with tasajo, malanga, boniato, yuca,
plantain and squash, and Fernando Ortiz used it as the metaphor for Cuba itself.
The bean distinction is the tell: moros y cristianos is black beans and rice
cooked in the same pot, congrí is red beans, and arroz con frijoles is cooked
separately. Tostones, maduros and mariquitas are three different things. Café
con leche with a pressed buttered tostada dunked in it is breakfast at any hour,
which is the point for a destination set in the small hours; Coppelia, the ice
cream cathedral on Calle 23, is open around the clock and sells in pesos.
Havana's seasons are dry (November to April) and wet (May to October); the
winter and summer tables above are that split. Avocado runs nearly year-round
here because the varieties are staged, and it is eaten as a salad with salt and
lime and nothing else.

Drawn on: California's own commercial season calendar, which settles several
things. Dungeness crab in the Monterey districts runs 15 November to 30 June, so
June is genuinely the last weeks of it and winter is its height. Spot prawns are
closed the whole of May, June and July — they cannot be a June dish. Chanterelles
are a winter mushroom here; the Big Sur chanterelle cook-off is held in
February. California halibut opens to the trawl fleet on 16 June. Salmon is the
trap: the commercial ocean fishery was closed outright for three straight years
and the reopened season is staggered, so June sits between windows — halibut is
the safer fish and the menu above uses it.

The strongest place-grounded protein on this coast is abalone from the Monterey
Abalone Company, red abalone grown in cages hung under Municipal Wharf No. 2. It
is farmed, so June is fine, and it turns up both as a Sierra Mar purveyor and on
its tasting menu, and on one-starred Chez Noir's menu in Carmel. Sierra Mar
prints its purveyors, which is the most useful single document here: Big Sur Sea
Salts, Ad Astra Bread in Monterey, Monterey Bay Seaweeds at Moss Landing, The
Cheese Shop in Carmel. Nepenthe, open since 1949 and the latest kitchen on the
coast at ten, names Gilroy garlic and Point Reyes blue on its own card; the Big
Sur River Inn names Castroville outright in a pasta; Deetjen's serves beef from
Lonely Bull Cattle Ranch in Big Sur itself. Strawberries are everywhere on
Central Coast menus in June. Wild fennel grows the length of Highway 1.

Two facts worth holding: the Big Sur Bakery has been closed since a kitchen fire
in May 2024 and has not reopened, though its website still advertises hours. And
after ten at night there is effectively nothing open on this coast — that is not
a gap in the pool, it is the character of the place, and it is why there is no
late supper proposed here.

Drawn on: what is verifiably open at three in the morning, which is a shorter
list than reputation suggests. The Clover Grill on Bourbon, open since 1939, says
plainly on its own site that it runs around the clock. Melba's on Elysian Fields
is genuinely 24/7 and shares its building with a working laundromat. Verti Marte
on Royal is 24/7 with a note asking you to call ahead very late. Buffa's front
bar never closes. Café du Monde has sold nothing but beignets and chicory café au
lait since 1862. Coop's Place, long famous as a late kitchen, now closes at ten
or eleven — the reputation is out of date.

The dish is yakamein, called old sober here: stewed brisket in beef broth over
spaghetti with half a hard-boiled egg and green onion, sold at second lines and
out of coolers, championed since 2006 by Linda Green. It is prescribed locally
as a hangover cure and is the actual 3 a.m. food of this city. Beside it, the
fried oyster loaf, whose other name is la médiatrice, the peacemaker — the
sandwich a husband brings home to the person he kept waiting. A dish named for
the hour you eat it. Calas, the rice fritters that Creole women sold at dawn
calling "Bel calas, tout chauds," are the ancestor the beignet eclipsed; putting
them at the end of a night rather than beignets is an argument, and a good one.

Roast beef po-boys with debris — the meat that falls into the gravy in the pan,
which Mother's menu defines in exactly those words — from Parkway and Domilise's,
on Leidenheimer bread, which is built with less flour and more water than a
baguette precisely because the Gulf climate would not grow wheat. "Dressed"
means lettuce, tomato, pickles and mayonnaise, and outsiders forget the pickle.
Barbecue shrimp is Pascal's Manale's and there is no barbecue in it: head-on
shrimp in peppery butter with Worcestershire and lemon, and the bread is the
utensil. Char-grilled oysters are Drago's. Red beans on Monday is a labour
schedule, not a flourish — ham was Sunday, Monday was washday, and a pot of beans
could sit on the stove while the washing got done. Casamento's closes June
through August rather than serve a summer oyster, which is the cleanest possible
statement of the R-month rule and is why menu 73 is a fall menu. Crawfish is
farmed in the rice fields and runs roughly winter into June; Creole tomatoes are
early summer; snowballs at Hansen's run March to October. Galatoire's, Antoine's,
Commander's Palace, Dooky Chase and Willie Mae's are the dressed and Sunday
registers the existing menus already cover.

Drawn on: the new oil is the off-season event on this coast — Taggiasca olives
are harvested November to February and milled the same day, and Riviera di
Levante is Portofino's own sub-zone of the DOP. Farinata is explicitly winter
street food: chickpea batter rested hours, poured into a tinned copper pan, ten
minutes in a wood oven at 300°C, black pepper at the table, eaten the moment it
comes out. It is sold in Genoa's sciamadde, the old fire shops — Antica Sà
Pesta, founded 1889, and Il Genovese, which still has a 1912 wood oven — along
with panissa, torte di verdura and frisceu. Cappon magro is the canonical
cold-weather showpiece and a Christmas Eve and Good Friday dish: layers of
separately cooked vegetables and fish over hardtack rubbed with garlic and
moistened with vinegar, green sauce between every layer, built as a tower.
Stoccafisso accomodato and buridda are the winter preserved-fish stews. Cima is
made ahead, pressed under weights and served cold, sliced. Pandolce genovese
carries the laurel sprig and a slice kept back for San Biagio on 3 February.
Antica Osteria di Vico Palla in Genoa has the deepest surviving version of this
repertoire. Puny on the piazza in Portofino will do cappon magro on request and
keeps salted anchovies with butter and crostini on the card. Da Giovanni at San
Fruttuoso, reachable only by boat or on foot, is open year-round.

Pesto is not off-limits in winter: Genoese DOP basil is grown in soil under
glass year-round and the world mortar championship is held in March. Trofie are
boiled with the potatoes and green beans in the same pot, which is the detail
outsiders get wrong.

# Observations on the existing menus

Listed, not fixed.

**Portofino is authored as off-season and has no off-season menu.** Menus 34,
35 and 36 are Summer, Spring or summer, and Summer. Nothing in the pool matches
the register the destination is named for. This is the largest single mismatch
in the file and the reason all three Portofino proposals are winter.

**New Orleans is authored at 3 a.m. and has no late-night menu.** Menu 33 is a
late brunch and 32 is a dressed dinner. The hour the destination is built on is
not represented, and the food that actually exists at that hour there — yakamein,
the roast beef po-boy with gravy, the fried oyster loaf, chicory coffee — is not
in the pool.

**Menu 21 ends a brisket dinner with cheese blintzes.** In the Borscht Belt
register a meat dinner carries no dairy: the hotels were kosher, Jennie
Grossinger ran the Grossinger's kitchen personally, and blintzes, sour cream and
herring belong to breakfast, lunch and the tea room. The desserts that end a
meat dinner are pareve — sponge cake, strudel, compote, sherbet. Menu 21 may be
a deliberate non-kosher reading, but if the destination is meant to carry the
Borscht Belt tradition, this is the one line that gives it away.

**Menu 23 serves roast pork in the Catskills.** As an upstate October cabin
dinner it is unimpeachable. Against the Borscht Belt reading it is the single
excluded meat. Worth deciding which register the destination is in, because
menus 21 and 23 are currently in different ones.

**Peconic Bay scallops never appear in Westhampton.** They are the East End's
signature shellfish and in 1976 the fishery was intact and enormous — the brown
tide collapse is 1985–88. The season is fall and winter, so menu 3 (Fall) is
their natural home and is conspicuously without them.

**Menu 8 sits on the opening of Nantucket bay scallop season and omits them.**
The family fishery opens October 1 and opening day is an island event. The
menus are right to keep scallops out of August, but October is when they belong.

**Menu 17 lists a raw seafood platter for high summer on the Côte d'Azur.** If
that platter is meant to include sea urchins, the season is wrong and the
sourcing is illegal locally: urchin fishing is prohibited in the
Alpes-Maritimes from April to December. Oysters, clams, prawns and langoustines
on the same platter are fine.

**Menu 34's trofie with pesto is missing the potatoes and green beans.** In
Liguria they are boiled in the same pot as the pasta and the dish is not the
same dish without them. This is the detail that reads as local rather than as
Italian.

**Menu 10 should be specific about the caviar in the shopping edit.** Beluga has
been banned for import into the US since 2005, with one domestically farmed
exception. Ossetra, kaluga hybrid or American farmed roe are what a host can
actually buy.

**Vegas has no seasonal or making variation at all.** Both menus are Year-round
and both are Actually made. The steakhouse and midnight-breakfast registers are
exactly right for 1968 — the tableside Caesar and the flamed cherries in menu
19 are period-perfect — but the destination was unavailable to a host who
answered "everything arrives finished."

**Cap Ferrat has no "actually made" menu and the Dolomites has no "bought and
arranged" one.** Each destination was invisible to one of the three answers a
host gives on the making question.

**Tahiti and Havana have no daytime menu between them.** Four menus, all dinner,
late supper or beachside. Havana in particular has no breakfast, which is odd
for a destination whose whole premise is the small hours — café con leche and a
buttered tostada are what is eaten at the end of the night.

**Menu 33 serves oysters at a brunch marked "Any" season.** New Orleans keeps
the R-month rule as a working calendar, not a superstition — Casamento's simply
closes June through August rather than serve a summer oyster. Bay oysters spawn
from the end of June into mid-August and the meat goes thin and milky, and Gulf
oysters carry high bacterial loads in warm water. The season on this menu should
probably be fall through spring, or the oysters should come out of the summer
version.

**Menu 30's blackberry crisp is a summer dessert on a fall menu.** California
blackberries run July into September. The menu says "Fall, works year-round",
which mostly absorbs it, but a fall Big Sur dessert is more likely apples,
pears or persimmons.

**Menu 25's dense chocolate cake is the one Dolomites dish with no local
claim.** Everything else on 24 and 25 is exact. Strudel, Kaiserschmarrn,
Buchteln, Strauben and Zelten are the region's own; a Sacher-style chocolate
cake is defensible as Habsburg but is the weakest line in two otherwise
faultless menus.

**Menu 2's cheese fondue is period-correct rather than place-correct.** For 1976
American entertaining it is exactly right and probably deliberate. Noting it
only so it does not get read as a Long Island dish later.

**Menu 1's cold potato-leek soup is a New York invention, not a Long Island
one** — Louis Diat at the Ritz-Carlton, around 1917. It is entirely correct for
a 1976 East End table. Noted for the record, not as a problem.
