### Cut, and why

**Pozole, in every form, and this is the calibration the founder set.** Her
ruling: *"Pozole verde is Guerrero; pozole generally is Jalisco-and-north.
Putting it in Oaxaca is Mexico-generic — the ceviche-in-Amalfi error wearing a
pot."* I looked for the evidence that would overturn it and found something more
interesting than nothing: **there IS a rooted Oaxacan pozole, and it is not
valley.** Larousse's dictionary entry names *pozole mixteco* — white pork pozole
with garlic, onion and hierba santa, mixed with mole colorado at serving — and
locates the distinctive version at San Juan Bautista Coixtlahuaca and generally
in the **Mixteca**. So the honest verdict is narrower and firmer than the
founder's: pozole in Oaxaca is Mixteca food, and this room is the Valles
Centrales. It stays cut. **The door, named so she can open it:** if she ever
wants pozole in the catalogue as Oaxacan, it comes in as Mixteca, on that
citation, and it does not become valley food by being in the same state.

**Caldo de piedra — cut, and it was one of her own leads.** Her note offered it:
*"tlayudas, memelas, quesillo, caldo de piedra, chapulines if you're brave."*
Every other item on that list is below. This one is not, because the sources are
unanimous and specific: it is **Chinantec**, from San Felipe Usila in the
Papaloapan basin, and Larousse files it under Sierra Norte (Chinanteco), not
under the valley. INPI and the state both call it the dish of the Chinantec
people. It is a stone-heated broth cooked in a gourd at a riverbank by men, for
the whole community — magnificent, and four mountain ranges from this courtyard.
Cutting a founder lead needs the reason written down, so: **structure-fit is
exactly why it is tempting** — a communal pot, an old technique, a thing that
takes a village — and rule 30's sibling trap says structure-fit is how
wrong-region dishes arrive. Hers to overrule, and if she does, it should be
recorded as the Papaloapan and not as the valley.

**Totopos and garnachas — Isthmus.** Larousse's Oaxaca entry splits the
tortillas explicitly: *"Tlayudas (Valle Central), totopos (Istmo)."* Garnachas
are the Juchitán antojito. Rule 6 says `regional` travels within its cuisine and
not its country; the Isthmus is not the valley any more than Ligurian is
Campanian, and the split here is drawn by the source itself.

**Tetelas — Mixteca.** Larousse's memela entry: in the Mixteca region shared by
Puebla and Oaxaca they are called tetelas. A triangular masa parcel is a
beautiful thing and it belongs to a region this room is not.

**Chileajo.** Larousse calls it *"típico de Guerrero y Oaxaca"* and then names
its Oaxacan forms at Juchitán, in the Mixteca, and at Huajuapan de León — three
attributions, none of them the Valles Centrales. Cut for want of positive valley
evidence, not because it is wrong.

**Entomatadas and enfrijoladas.** Documented Oaxacan — both are in Larousse's
Oaxaca entry — and cut anyway, on rule 30. Tortillas dipped in tomato sauce or
bean sauce is a form the whole country makes and no other room's plate would
have to give it up. It buys this room no distinction, which is the entire reason
the two Mexican rooms need separate food.

**Barbacoa.** Larousse lists it under Oaxaca with no regional qualifier, and the
strong national attribution is central Mexico. No valley source found. Held out
rather than argued in.

**Guacamole, and the same reasoning Acapulco used.** Legal as repertoire, cut
because it would sit identically well on both Mexican tables.

**Cocadas.** Acapulco's file already flagged this as the first collision the day
somebody writes Oaxacan food, and warned it correctly: *"Oaxaca has a coast, and
cocadas are made on it."* This pass is the valley. Cocada stays Acapulco's, and
the collision it predicted has not happened because I did not go to the coast.

**Mole poblano, chiles en nogada.** Puebla.

**Flan.** Acapulco's file already reasoned that this room's ending has nowhere to

**The market grill as a scene, and the ice-cream garden as a place.** Both fail
period and both fail rule 25.2 anyway. The grilling alley of the city market is
dated by the local press to *"more than six decades"* as of the mid-2020s —

not; the nieve survives and the garden does not.** Also recorded because it is
the kind of thing that gets written in by accident: the city's wholesale market
opened in **1978**. In 1954 a valley household's marketing world is the two
nineteenth-century covered markets — 1882 and 1894 — and nothing else.

### What could not be dated or attributed confidently

- **"Los siete moles" as a framing.** The individual moles are old; the sevenfold
  grouping is not datable from anything I could find. Spanish Wikipedia states
  the framing and gives no attribution and no date. Every valley source that
  uses it is modern. My reading is that it is a later-twentieth-century culinary
  and tourism formulation over genuinely older dishes, and I could not prove
  it either way. **Consequence for the copy, and it is a real one:** a 1954
  kitchen would not say "the seven moles". It would say *the mole*, which is
  exactly what her premise and her exemplars already say. The seven are seven
  DISH ROWS; they are not a phrase this room utters.
- **Which dish is the seventh.** Two Mexican sources give **estofado** as the
  seventh and two give **manchamanteles**. Both are documented Oaxacan; both are
  written below as separate lines rather than adjudicated. Manchamanteles
  additionally carries a live Oaxaca/Puebla origin dispute and appears in Sor
  Juana's seventeenth-century recetario, which is period-safe but not
  Oaxaca-proving.
- **The loaded tlayuda.** The word is traceable: an 1890 Oaxacan novel uses
  *tlayudas* as an ADJECTIVE for tough tortillas; the term nominalises into *la
  tlayuda* in the 1920s–30s; a **1933** magazine already describes it as a large
  specially-made tortilla reheated and covered with asiento and salsa. That is
  the version written below and it is secure for 1954. What I could NOT date to
  before 1954 is the fully-loaded restaurant tlayuda with quesillo and tasajo as
  fixed components — that dating rests on popular sources, and one valley
  account dates the large-tortilla trade of its own town to roughly the 1960s.
- **Quesillo's origin story.** The 1885 accident by a fourteen-year-old girl at
  Reyes Etla is oral tradition and no record of her has been found. The real
  evidence is better for our purpose anyway: cheese-making on Etla-valley
  haciendas is documented from the mid-eighteenth century, 1890s railway
  shipping receipts show quesillo moving to Puebla and Mexico City, small-scale
  commercial quesillo at Reyes Etla is documented by 1915, and the commercial
  expansion runs from the late 1930s through the 1950s. In 1954 it is not just
  present, it is in the middle of its expansion.
- **Chapulines in the city market before 1954.** Consumption is documented from
  Sahagún in the sixteenth century and the continuity is not seriously in doubt,
  but I could not pin a pre-1954 citation to the Oaxaca CITY market stalls
  specifically, as opposed to regional consumption.
- **The age of the nieve craft.** Press accounts claim six generations and more
  than a hundred years, which would put it comfortably before 1954, and none of
  it is archival.
- **Molotes' valley origin.** Larousse lists molotes among the representative
  Oaxacan antojitos, which is solid. The Zapotec-valley origin claim is popular
  sourcing, and other sources treat the molote as shared with Puebla.
- **A printed pre-1954 Oaxacan source.** Josefina Velázquez de León's *Platillos
  Regionales de la República Mexicana* is confirmed at **1946** — the same author
  whose book carries Acapulco's ceviche — but I could not confirm that its
  contents include Oaxaca. Her *Cocina Oaxaqueña* exists and the only edition I
  could date is a 1984 second edition, so it must NOT be cited as pre-1954.
  Malinowski and de la Fuente's valley market study rests on **1940–41**
  fieldwork and was published in Spanish in 1957; Beals's valley marketing study
  is later. All three are the right sources for someone who can read the physical
  texts, and none of them yielded named dishes through what I could reach.
- **Season on gusanos de maguey**, which is why they appear only inside the
  worm-salt line and not as a dish of their own.
- **Whether tuna and jiotilla seasons bind**, flagged at the two nieve lines.

## The borders, stated once

Three walls, and every line below is checked against all three.

### 1. Against ACAPULCO, 1959 — the mandatory line

Acapulco's file states it and it is quoted verbatim because it is the test:
**heat and masa on one side, cold and lime on the other.**

Oaxaca's food is hot and made of corn. The mole started days ago, the tortillas
came off a comal this morning, the tamales are in the steamer, and the stove's
only job tonight is keeping it warm. **The kitchen is the speaker and it has
authority.** Acapulco has no kitchen in its voice at all — things appear, nobody
serves them, nothing was cooked by anybody who is still cooking, and what
seasons a dish is lime.

Each dish below carries a **why not Acapulco** line and it is not decoration:
the two rooms score 0.101 on tone affinity, which is the widest gap this room
has to any warm room, and rule 26's second number — deliverables overlap — is
mute until both rooms have food. It stops being mute when this lands.

### 2. Against generic MEXICO — the harder one

The founder's pozole ruling is the calibration and the rule is stated as a
procedure, not a sentiment: **rootedness by evidence, never resemblance of
form.** A dish does not belong here because it is a pot that simmers or a thing
made of masa. Lots of things are pots and half the country is masa. It belongs
here because a source places it in Oaxaca, and preferably in the valley.

Applied honestly, that cut three dishes this pass that a lazier reading would
have kept — entomatadas, enfrijoladas and barbacoa are all Oaxaca-adjacent and
all of them are shapes rather than roots — and it cut pozole a second time on a
firmer citation than the founder had.

### 3. Against Oaxaca's own other regions — the wall inside the state

This is the one the founder predicted would trip me, and it did the most work.
**Oaxaca is not one cuisine.** Larousse's own entry sorts its dishes into
Valles Centrales, Istmo, Costa, Mixteca, Cañada/Papaloapan and two Sierras, and
the differences between them are larger than the differences between some of
this catalogue's countries. **This room is the VALLEY** — her premise's
courtyard, comal and long table, and the mole that started yesterday.

So: totopos and garnachas are the Isthmus, tetelas and chileajo and pozole are
the Mixteca, caldo de piedra is the Chinantla, cocadas and pescado are the
coast. Six cuts, all of them from inside the same state, and each one is the
ceviche-in-Amalfi error with a Oaxacan passport.

**THE COAST IS THE ONE TO SAY OUT LOUD, because Acapulco's file predicted this
exact collision and named it in advance.** Oaxaca has six hundred kilometres of
Pacific coastline with a full seafood cuisine of its own — Larousse's Costa
section lists octopus salad, shrimp broths, smoked and adobo'd fish, *pescado a
la talla*, seafood tamales — and every one of those dishes would break the
Acapulco border from behind while carrying a Oaxacan citation. **Not one coastal
Oaxacan dish is in this file.** The test applied to every line was not "is this
Oaxacan" but "is this the VALLEY", and the two are not the same question. If
somebody later writes coastal Oaxaca as its own room, that room and Acapulco are
the pair that will need this whole exercise repeated — and it will be harder,
because they share an ocean. Where a dish is Oaxacan but
travels within Oaxaca — mole amarillo is also Mazatec, chepil is also coastal —
it is tiered `regional` rather than `signature`, on that evidence.

### Orange slices with worm salt, off the mezcal plate
- **course:** appetizer
- **tier:** regional — Oaxacan. Argued: sal de gusano is not made anywhere else
  in Mexico in any quantity, but it belongs to the mezcal rather than to the
  valley alone, and mezcal's country is larger than this room. `signature` would
  claim an exclusive the state, not the valley, actually holds.
- **season:** none
- **making:** none. The salt is bought or is in the house; the orange is cut.
- **why this room:** it is the first thing that happens and nobody hands it to
  you. It sits beside the clay cup, and her lexicon has already said the only
  thing this house says about mezcal: *the vessel is the fact.*
- **why not Acapulco:** it is the accompaniment to a spirit poured in clay in a
  courtyard. Acapulco's seasoning is lime and its drink is not this one.
- **period:** secure and pinned. A 1931 UNAM thesis on the *chilocuiles* of
  Oaxaca describes communities at Mitla and Ocotlán — both valley — using the
  worms to flavour the local mezcals. That is twenty-three years before the room
  and it is the earliest specific written valley-foodways source I found.
- **founder question:** none. This line already exists in `docs/dishes.md` and
  the research confirms it rather than changing it.

### Chapulines, toasted with garlic and chile, and a lime
- **course:** appetizer
- **tier:** regional — Oaxacan. Argued: chapulines are eaten in Guerrero and
  central Mexico too, so `signature` overclaims; they do not travel to any other
  room in this catalogue, so `repertoire` would let them somewhere they have no
  business.
- **season:** **summer, and it binds.** The swarm comes with the first rains —
  the eggs hatch in May, the fields fill from late May to September, and the
  harvest and market season runs June to December. This is a rainy-season food
  and cannot honestly be written as a year-round line.
- **making:** toasted on a comal with garlic, chile and lime and put in a bowl.
  The bowl is refilled by whoever notices.
- **why this room:** it is her own lead — *"chapulines if you're brave"* — and it
  is the one food on the table that a guest has to decide about. Nobody explains
  it to you and nobody watches you do it, which is this room's manners exactly.
- **why not Acapulco:** it is dry, toasted, from a field, and it goes with a
  spirit rather than with the sea. Nothing about it is cold and nothing about it
  is lime-cooked.
- **period:** secure by a long margin. Consumption is documented by Sahagún in
  the sixteenth century and the practice is continuous. See the flag above: I
  could not pin the CITY market specifically before 1954.
- **founder question:** the room is dated only by year, not by month, so a
  `season: summer` line has no premise month to fight — unlike Acapulco's mango,
  which fought December. Does she want the season recorded on the line anyway,
  given that rule 25.1 books the room in a backyard in August and August is
  exactly when this is right?

### Tostadas with chintextle, the chile paste ground on the metate
- **course:** appetizer
- **tier:** regional — Oaxacan.
- **season:** none
- **making:** the paste is made ahead and keeps: pasilla oaxaqueño chiles smoked
  and toasted, then ground on a metate with a little water and salt. The
  valley-town version adds guajillo, garlic, green tomato and sometimes dried
  shrimp. Spread on a tostada whenever.
- **why this room:** it is a thing that was ground by hand before anybody came
  and it is the room's heat said the way she wants heat said — an ingredient,
  never a headline. Her `never` list is explicit: *"Never spicy as a headline."*
- **why not Acapulco:** a metate, a comal and a paste that keeps. There is no
  grinding stone in a room with no kitchen.
- **period:** the technique and the chile are old and unremarkable; the pasilla
  oaxaqueño is the state's own chile. No dating flag.
- **founder question:** the name at the table. "Chintextle" is a table word in
  the valley and a guest in Brooklyn would not say it. Does rule 25.2 admit it
  on the strength of the first, or does the line become "the chile paste, on a
  tostada"? Same question Acapulco's morisqueta raised, and it should be answered
  once for both rooms.

### Quesillo, pulled apart at the table
- **course:** appetizer
- **tier:** regional — Oaxacan. Argued, and it is a close call. The cheese
  carries the state's name and would be a good `signature`; but by 1954 it is
  already a national commercial product moving on the railway, and this
  catalogue's other rooms could plausibly hold it. `regional` is honest and keeps
  it inside Oaxacan cuisine, where rule 6 wants it.
- **season:** none
- **making:** none, and that is the point. It is bought in a ball and it is
  unwound by hand at the table, in strings, by whoever reaches it.
- **why this room:** nobody portions it and nobody plates it. It is a thing you
  take a piece of while somebody is still talking, and it is the only food in the
  room that is dismantled rather than served.
- **why not Acapulco:** it is a valley dairy product from a landlocked plain,
  eaten warm-ish and dry. There is no cheese in a room whose whole seasoning is
  lime.
- **period:** secure, and among the best-documented things here. Cheese-making
  on Etla-valley haciendas from the mid-eighteenth century; 1890s railway
  receipts; commercial quesillo at Reyes Etla by 1915; expansion through the
  1930s–1950s. The 1885 girl is folklore and is not relied on.
- **founder question:** none.

### Memelas, pinched at the rim, with asiento and beans
- **course:** appetizer
- **tier:** regional — southern Mexican. Argued: Larousse records the same thing
  as *memeles* in the Sierra de Puebla and as *tetelas* in the Mixteca, so this
  is a form with a wide range, and `signature` would be a false exclusive. The
  VALLEY version — oval, pinched at the edge, cooked on one side and turned — is
  what the line describes.
- **season:** none
- **making:** masa, a comal, and somebody standing at it. Larousse's valley entry
  says they are eaten at breakfast, as an antojito at any hour, as a first course
  at the midday meal, or as a plain supper — which is four different times of day
  and is why this is the room's most flexible line.
- **why this room:** a comal going while people arrive is the sound of this
  premise. Rule 25.3 was amended today precisely so a line like this does not
  have to pretend it was finished hours ago.
- **why not Acapulco:** masa, a comal, and lard. Three things that room does not
  own, in one object.
- **period:** secure. The form is pre-Hispanic and continuous.
- **founder question:** none.

### Empanadas de amarillo, folded over on the comal
- **course:** appetizer
- **tier:** signature. Argued: Larousse's entry places these in the Valles
  Centrales and the valley's own food press does the same; the amarillo inside is
  one of the room's own moles. Nothing else in this catalogue can take it.
- **season:** none
- **making:** an oval raw tortilla on the comal, cooked one side, turned, filled
  with shredded chicken and thick mole amarillo and a whole sprig of cilantro,
  folded into a half-moon and pinched shut with the fingers as it finishes.
- **why this room:** it is the mole appearing at breakfast because there was mole
  — the pot that never empties, said as an object you can hold. And it is made in
  front of you by somebody who does not stop the conversation to do it.
- **why not Acapulco:** it is masa closed around a mole. Both halves are the wall.
- **period:** secure. A comal antojito of the valley, and the amarillo it holds
  is one of the old moles.
- **founder question:** none.

### Molotes, fried, with potato and chorizo inside
- **course:** appetizer
- **tier:** regional — southern Mexican. Argued down from signature: Larousse
  names molotes among the representative Oaxacan antojitos, which is good
  evidence, but other sources treat the molote as shared with Puebla and the
  Zapotec-valley origin claim is popular rather than documentary.
- **season:** none
- **making:** masa rolled around potato and chorizo, fried until it cracks, put
  on a plate with lettuce, cheese and a bean sauce.
- **why this room:** it is the food that turns up in quantity without anybody
  announcing that food is turning up, and it is eaten with fingers by people who
  are still arguing about a year in a story.
- **why not Acapulco:** fried masa. That room's fried thing came out of the water.
- **period:** no dating flag on the form; see the attribution flag above.
- **founder question:** does she want a line whose valley attribution is good and
  whose origin claim is popular, or is a Larousse listing under Oaxaca enough?
  This is the weakest attribution in the appetizers and it is worth her seeing.

### Chicatana salsa, ground in the molcajete
- **course:** appetizer
- **tier:** regional — Oaxacan and Guerreran. Argued: sources place the chicatana
  across Valles Centrales, the Mixteca and the Costa, and in Guerrero; it is not
  a valley exclusive, but it does not leave the south either.
- **season:** **early summer, and it binds hard.** The winged ants come out with
  the first rains — the two weeks around the start of June, sometimes late May to
  mid-July — once or twice a year, and they are gathered before dawn. This is the
  most tightly seasonal line in the file.
- **making:** the ants toasted, then ground on a molcajete or metate with dried
  chile, garlic, salt and a little water. Put down with tortillas, or spooned
  over anything that came off the comal.
- **why this room:** it is time-depth as a fact about a calendar rather than
  about a person — the joke this room makes is the length of the clock, and this
  is a food that exists for two weeks a year and has for a very long time.
- **why not Acapulco:** it is a ground salsa from a valley field, made on a stone,
  from something that only appears when the rains come. That room's timekeeping
  is a pile of shells.
- **period:** secure. The practice is ancient and continuous, and the salsa is
  named in the Mexican traditional-cuisine dossier.
- **founder question:** a dish available two weeks a year is the narrowest season
  in the catalogue. Does the schema's `season` field want `summer`, or does this
  line need a narrower value than the field can hold?

### Quesadillas of squash blossom, on the comal
- **course:** appetizer
- **tier:** regional — southern Mexican. The quesadilla is national; the filling
  and the comal are the valley's.
- **season:** **summer.** The blossom is a rainy-season crop and belongs with the
  same milpa that gives this room its sopa de guías.
- **making:** masa on the comal, blossom and quesillo folded in, turned once.
- **why this room:** it is the second thing the comal does while it is already
  hot, which is the room's whole economy of effort — nobody lights anything
  twice.
- **why not Acapulco:** masa and melted cheese, hot, at a comal.
- **period:** secure; the blossom and the form are both pre-Hispanic in origin.
- **founder question:** this and the memela are the same technique at two
  fillings, the way Acapulco's two garlic-shrimp lines were the same technique at
  two sizes. She may want one, not both.

### Tortillas, made this morning, still warm if you come early enough
- **course:** appetizer
- **tier:** signature. Argued: the tortilla is the most generic object in Mexican
  food and this is not a tortilla line — it is a line about a MORNING, and the
  clause that makes it hers is *if you come early enough.* No other room can take
  that sentence.
- **season:** none
- **making:** masa and a comal, this morning, in quantity. They go into a cloth
  in a basket and stay warm there.
- **why this room:** the food is the only thing presented, and this is the thing
  the presenting is done WITH. Everything else on the table is picked up with it.
- **why not Acapulco:** masa is the wall. Acapulco's own file says so twice and
  cut a documented 1950s dish — shark tamales — rather than cross it.
- **period:** not a period claim.
- **founder question:** none. This line already exists and is hers.

## Mains

### The mole, going since yesterday
- **course:** main
- **tier:** signature. The room's dish and the room's schedule. It never travels.
- **season:** none
- **making:** days. Sources put mole negro at thirty-plus ingredients — chilhuacle
  negro, mulato and pasilla chiles, chocolate, bread, plantain, tomato, almonds,
  seeds and spices, each element toasted separately — and say plainly that it
  takes days of work and a costly volume of ingredients, which is what makes it
  the dish of the big feast. Nothing about this needs shortening and rule 25.3
  no longer asks it to.
- **why this room:** it IS the room. Her premise's first clause is this dish and
  her humour's whole mechanism is its clock: *your grandmother would have started
  Wednesday.* Somebody is sent to stir it mid-sentence and the sentence carries
  on without them.
- **why not Acapulco:** a pot that has been going since yesterday is the exact
  thing that room cannot contain. Its file cut its own most-likely dish partly
  on the grounds that a long pot is Oaxaca's structure and two Mexican rooms
  cannot both be a simmering pot.
- **period:** the deepest evidence in the file. The seventeenth-century Dominican
  chronicler Burgoa describes a ritual *totolmole* — turkey cooked with chilhuacle
  chile, squash seeds and hierba santa — offered at indigenous funeral
  ceremonies, which is this dish's documented ancestor. The occasion set is
  weddings, baptisms, Day of the Dead and mayordomías.
- **founder question:** the LINE, not the dish. This one already exists as *"The
  mole, going since yesterday"* and it is right. The question is whether the pool
  now carries both this line AND a separate `mole negro` row, or whether this one
  line is the negro and the other six are named beside it. My reading is the
  first — her line is the room's signature and the taxonomy sits under it — but
  it is a schema decision and it is hers.

### Mole coloradito
- **course:** main
- **tier:** regional — Oaxacan.
- **season:** none
- **making:** ancho and pasilla chiles, tomato, almonds, oregano and a little
  sugar, ground and cooked down to a brick red. It goes with pork, chicken or
  beef.
- **why this room:** it is the one that gets made when there is no reason to make
  the black one, which is a sentence about how often this house makes mole.
- **why not Acapulco:** a ground, cooked-down sauce over meat, served hot from a
  pot.
- **period:** secure as part of the old mole complex; see the flag on the
  sevenfold framing, which is a NAME problem and not a dish problem.
- **founder question:** none.

### Mole amarillo, with chochoyotes in it
- **course:** main
- **tier:** regional — Oaxacan. Argued: Larousse records mole amarillo in the
  Mazateca as well as the valley, so it travels within Oaxaca. That is exactly
  what `regional` means under rule 6 and it is why this is not `signature`.
- **season:** none
- **making:** yellow chilhuacle and hierba santa, which gives it its anise note,
  with chicken or pork and chayote, green beans and potatoes. The chochoyotes —
  masa dumplings, thumb-pressed — go in near the end.
- **why this room:** it is a mole that is also a pot of vegetables, so it is the
  one that feeds the cousins of cousins who came through the door late. The
  dumplings are made from the same masa as the tortillas, by the same hands, that
  morning.
- **why not Acapulco:** masa dumplings in a hot pot. Two walls at once.
- **period:** secure. Hierba santa is in Burgoa's seventeenth-century description
  of the ancestor dish.
- **founder question:** I have chochoyotes firmly sourced into sopa de guías and
  commonly stated for amarillo. Keep them on this line, or move them to the soup
  only?

### Mole verde

### Chichilo negro — CUT BY FOUNDER RULING, NOT LANDED

**"no funeral mole."** This block is kept whole under rule 14 and is the one of
the thirty-seven that is not in `docs/dishes.md`. Everything below it is the
evidence that made the case FOR it, and the ruling beat the evidence. Do not
reinstate the dish from this block; if it is ever reopened it is reopened by
her, not by a reader finding the argument still persuasive.

- **course:** main
- **tier:** regional — Oaxacan, and the rarest thing in the file.
- **season:** none
- **making:** pasilla and mulato chiles with **burnt tortilla** ground into it,
  and avocado leaves, over beef. The burnt tortilla is the whole character and
  it is the only mole that does that.
- **why this room:** the sources call it the *mole triste* — the mole of the
  wake, made for velorios and novenarios to see the dead off. That is time-depth
  at its most serious, and this room's humour is about the length of the clock
  precisely because the clock is what the house actually keeps. A room that says
  *since before you were born* has a dish for that sentence.
- **why not Acapulco:** a room with no kitchen has no mourning dish, and would
  not want one. This is the furthest apart the two rooms get.
- **period:** secure within the mole complex.
- **founder question:** **this one needs her.** A funeral mole on a Sunday table
  is either the deepest line in the room or a wrong note, and the answer is a
  taste call, not a research call. It is also the one dish here whose occasion
  cannot be softened without lying about it.

### Manchamanteles
- **course:** main
- **tier:** regional — and argued down, honestly, because of a live dispute.
  Sources split on whether it is Oaxacan or Pueblan; the Pueblan legend is a
  convent story, and it appears in Sor Juana's seventeenth-century recetario.
  Some Oaxacan sources count it among the seven and some substitute estofado.
- **season:** none
- **making:** chilhuacle rojo and guajillo with plantain, pineapple, peach,
  raisins and sweet potato, with pork. It is the sweet one and it stains the
  cloth, which is what its name says.
- **why this room:** a dish named after the mess it makes on a tablecloth belongs
  on a table that grows chairs. Nothing is protected here and nothing is
  performed.
- **why not Acapulco:** fruit cooked INTO a sauce rather than cut cold with salt
  on it. Same fruit, opposite room.
- **period:** the recipe is documented in a seventeenth-century Mexican
  manuscript, so period is safe. Region is the open question, not date.
- **founder question:** does a dish with a live Oaxaca/Puebla dispute belong in
  the pool at all, or is a contested attribution exactly the kind of thing rule 3
  says to flag rather than assert? My reading is that it goes in as `regional`
  with the dispute recorded here, because both claimants are named in Mexican
  sources and the dish is on the Oaxacan seven-list in two of them. Hers.

### Estofado, with almonds, olives and capers
- **course:** main
- **tier:** regional — Oaxacan. Argued: Larousse lists *estofado de res* under
  the Istmo as well, so it moves within the state.
- **season:** none
- **making:** the Spanish-descended one — olives, almonds, raisins, capers,
  cinnamon and clove — served with chicken, rice and pickled chiles with carrot.
- **why this room:** it is the dish that says the house has been cooking through
  four hundred years of somebody else's ingredients and stopped noticing which
  ones were imported. That is the room's tense — *since before* — as a plate.
- **why not Acapulco:** a braise with capers in it, hot, over rice.
- **period:** secure. It is the colonial layer of the mole complex.
- **founder question:** see the seventh-mole flag above. Estofado and
  manchamanteles are two answers to the same slot and this file writes both.

### Segueza
- **course:** main
- **tier:** signature. Argued at length, and it is the strongest border line in
  the file after the mole itself. Sources place it squarely in the Valles
  Centrales, its name is Zapotec — *sa* feast, *guezze* toasted corn — and it is
  made from broken yellow corn ground on the metate by the woman making it. It
  is not made anywhere else and it cannot travel.
- **season:** none
- **making:** yellow corn toasted and broken on a metate, not ground fine,
  because the texture is the point; then guajillo chile, the herb pitiona, and
  goat or chicken. It is not everyday food.
- **why this room:** it is a mole made of corn instead of chiles — the starch and
  the sauce are the same substance — and its own name means feast. Sources say it
  is made for weddings, wakes and patron-saint days. Her premise's Sunday IS the
  occasion this dish exists for.
- **why not Acapulco:** it is broken corn ground on a stone and simmered. Masa
  and the pot, together, in the one dish that is both.
- **period:** the Zapotec etymology and the metate technique put it well before
  the room; no source I found dates it, because nothing about it is datable.
- **founder question:** the name. "Segueza" is a table word in the valley and
  nowhere else on earth. Rule 25.2 again, and the same answer should serve
  chintextle, higaditos and morisqueta.

### Higaditos, the morning after
- **course:** main
- **tier:** signature. Sources put it in the Zapotec villages of the Valles
  Centrales specifically, under the names *higaditos de fandango* and *higaditos
  de mayordomía*. It goes nowhere.
- **season:** none
- **making:** the broth the mole was cooked in, with garlic, tomato, tomatillo,
  clove, cumin and saffron; turkey or chicken meat with liver and gizzards; then
  a great quantity of beaten egg poured in, which sets on the surface and is cut
  with the spoon into soft strands and served in bowls with a lot of broth.
- **why this room:** **this is the premise's other half and no other dish in the
  catalogue is.** It is what a valley village eats the morning after a wedding,
  cooked in the water the mole was cooked in, to feed everyone who stayed to
  help. Her tagline is *Sunday since yesterday*; this is the dish that is
  literally made out of yesterday.
- **why not Acapulco:** it is eggs set in a hot broth made from another dish's
  cooking liquid, served in bowls, in the morning. There is no morning in
  Acapulco's voice — there is only a lunch that never ended.
- **period:** secure; it is a village occasion dish with a Zapotec occasion name.
- **founder question:** this is the line I would most like her to see. Does the
  room want a dish whose whole identity is THE MORNING AFTER, when the premise
  is an afternoon that recedes at dusk? I think it is the best fit in the file
  and I am aware that is a strong claim.

### Tamales in banana leaf, with mole negro inside
- **course:** main
- **tier:** signature. Sources place the banana-leaf mole-negro tamal in the
  Valles Centrales; corn-husk is the northern and central form and the coast and
  Isthmus have their own fillings entirely.
- **season:** none
- **making:** a thin layer of masa, then the mole, then shredded chicken, turkey
  or pork, wrapped in banana leaf — larger, tougher and more fragrant than corn
  husk — and steamed. Made in quantity, because nobody makes four.
- **why this room:** her own exemplar is already the line: *Tamales, because there
  were always tamales.* This is the evidence under it. The steamer is the second
  pot and it is the one nobody has to be sent to stir.
- **why not Acapulco:** masa. Acapulco's file cut documented 1950s shark tamales
  from its own port rather than take this room's noun.
- **period:** secure.
- **founder question:** none. Her line stands; this block is its evidence.

### Tamales de chepil
- **course:** main
- **tier:** regional — Oaxacan. Argued: chepil grows across the southeast and the
  leaf is used on the coast too, so this is not a valley exclusive; but sources
  call the tamal itself typical of the Valles Centrales.
- **season:** none
- **making:** masa and lard with the fresh chepil leaves worked straight in, no
  filling. Steamed with the others.
- **why this room:** it is the tamal for the people who are not eating meat
  today, made without anybody discussing it or offering it as an alternative.
  Abundance stated as a fact about the pot, never as an offer to a person — her
  own `always` rule.
- **why not Acapulco:** masa with a herb in it, steamed.
- **period:** secure.
- **founder question:** none.

### Tamales de frijol
- **course:** main
- **tier:** regional — Oaxacan. Sources call it one of the most traditional
  tamales of the Oaxacan countryside without narrowing it to the valley.
- **season:** none
- **making:** ground beans and asiento worked into the masa. The plainest thing

- **why this room:** it is the tamal that is still there on Monday, which is the

### A tlayuda, with asiento and a chile salsa on it
- **course:** main
- **tier:** signature. Larousse's own tortilla list writes the split for me:
  *tlayudas (Valle Central), totopos (Istmo).* This is the valley's tortilla and
  the catalogue has nowhere else to put it.
- **season:** none
- **making:** the large hard tortilla — thirty centimetres and up — reheated and
  spread with asiento, the sediment from rendered pork fat, and dressed with a
  tomato or chile pasilla salsa. Black beans are the usual fourth thing and are
  named in the modern descriptions rather than in the period one, which is why
  the line above stops where it does. Folded, or torn.
- **why this room:** it is a plate that you eat. Her house measures in length —
  *the long table*, *more chairs* — and this is the only food in the catalogue
  whose defining property is its size.
- **why not Acapulco:** it is a tortilla the size of a hat, made of masa, put on
  a fire in a courtyard. Nothing in that sentence survives the border.
- **period:** documented, and the dating is the most interesting in the file.
  Three citations, verbatim from the Fundación Alfredo Harp Helú's study of the
  word. **1890**, as an ADJECTIVE for tough tortillas, in Arturo Fenochio Rosas's
  *El cielo de Oaxaca*: *"Aquí están las tortillas, algo tlayudas; pero no le
  hace."* **1926**, nominalised, in Eulogio R. Valdivieso in *Mexican Folkways*:
  *"la sabrosa clayuda (especie de tortilla que, después de cocerse por un lado
  en el comal…)."* And **1933**, in *El Mundo Gráfico*'s "La Cocina Oaxaqueña":
  *"Las personas más pobres pueden deleitarse tomando una tlayuda (tortilla
  grande, de manufactura especial) recalentada y cubierta con 'asiento' […] y
  rociada con salsa picante de tomate o de chile pasilla."* That last sentence is
  precisely this line and it is twenty-one years before the room. What is NOT
  claimed here is the fully-loaded modern version with quesillo and tasajo fixed
  on top — see the flag above, and note that the line deliberately stops at
  asiento and salsa for exactly that reason.
- **founder question:** none, and that restraint is deliberate: the line is
  written short because the evidence is short, not because the room is.

### Tasajo, over the coals, cut in long sheets
- **course:** main
- **tier:** regional — Oaxacan.
- **season:** none
- **making:** beef cut into long thin sheets — the verb is *tasajear* — salted
  and hung to dry in the open air with wood fires around it, which is where the
  smoke in it comes from. It needs no refrigeration, which is why it exists. On
  the coals for a minute or two and torn up.
- **why this room:** it is meat that was preserved months ago being cooked in two
  minutes, which is this room's two tenses in one object. And it goes on the same
  fire the comal is on.
- **why not Acapulco:** it is dried, salted, landlocked beef. That room's protein
  came out of the water an hour ago and its file says so on every line.
- **period:** secure. Salting and sun-drying beef is a colonial-era technique
  adopted in the Valles Centrales and the word's own lineage runs back through
  the Spanish of the Americas. **The city market's smoke alley, which is what a
  modern visitor pictures, is NOT period** — see the cuts. The meat is; the scene
  is not.
- **founder question:** none.

### Cecina enchilada
- **course:** main
- **tier:** regional — Oaxacan. Argued: cecina is a broad Mexican word, but in
  Oaxaca it specifically means the chile-rubbed PORK, orange-coloured, as against
  the salted beef, and that distinction is the room's.
- **season:** none
- **making:** pork in thin sheets rubbed with chile and spices, hung, then on the
  coals beside the tasajo. It can sit rubbed all afternoon and only improves.
- **why this room:** it is the second thing the fire does, and the fire was lit
  for the comal anyway. Nobody lights anything twice in this house.
- **why not Acapulco:** pork rubbed with dried chile and grilled inland.
- **period:** secure.
- **founder question:** this and the tasajo are the same gesture at two animals.
  She may want one.

### Sopa de guías, with chochoyotes
- **course:** main
- **tier:** signature. Sources name the Valles Centrales version explicitly,
  including the aromatic herb the valley adds to it and the pasilla or
  maguey-worm salsa and lime it is served with.
- **season:** **summer, and it binds.** The dish exists because the squash vine's
  tender shoots are only available in the rainy season; families describe it as
  the soup that announces the rains. A year-round line here would be false.
- **making:** the whole squash plant used at once — the shoots, the flowers, the
  young squash, the young corn — and masa dumplings pinched with a thumb and
  dropped in near the end. Served with a chile pasilla salsa and lime.
- **why this room:** it is the sopa aguada, the first course of the comida, and
  it is the milpa arriving whole. The pot is on and nobody is standing over it.
- **why not Acapulco:** a hot vegetable pot with masa dumplings in it, eaten with
  a spoon, in the rain.
- **period:** pre-Hispanic in origin; the tender shoots of the squash plant were
  eaten in Mesoamerica as ordinary food. No dating flag.
- **founder question:** none.

### Black beans from the pot, with an avocado leaf in it
- **course:** main
- **tier:** regional — southern Mexican. Argued: the pot of black beans is
  national and would be worthless as a line on its own — rule 30's exact warning.
  What makes it a claim is the toasted avocado leaf, which sources associate with
  Oaxaca and its neighbours and which puts an anise note into the pot.
- **season:** none
- **making:** the pot goes on early and stays on. Nothing else.
- **why this room:** frijoles is a COURSE in the comida, arriving after the
  guisado, and her lexicon already has the word for this exact object: *the pot
  that never empties.* Nobody is offered more; there is more.
- **why not Acapulco:** a pot that has been on all day, and a leaf toasted into
  it for a flavour nobody will name.
- **period:** secure.
- **founder question:** is the avocado leaf enough to make this a Oaxacan line
  rather than a Mexican one? I think so, and it is the closest call in the mains.

### Arroz con chepil
- **course:** main
- **tier:** regional — Oaxacan. Argued: the sopa seca of rice is national and
  would be pure generic; Larousse names *arroz con chepil* under Oaxaca
  specifically, and the herb is the whole distinction.
- **season:** none
- **making:** rice cooked with the fresh chepil leaves through it. It holds in
  its pot for the length of the afternoon.
- **why this room:** the comida has a rice course and this is the room's version
  of it. It is the plainest thing on the table and it goes under everything else.
- **why not Acapulco:** rice appears in BOTH rooms and this is where the border
  gets tested rather than asserted. Acapulco's rice is morisqueta — white,
  unseasoned, with beans and whatever came out of the water on top, and it
  arrived through that port on the galleons. This one is cooked WITH a valley
  herb and goes under a mole. Same grain, two countries of the plate, and the
  distinction is exactly the one rule 6 wants a cuisine border to make.
- **period:** rice is colonial and long established; the pairing with chepil is
  the Oaxacan part and is undated but unremarkable.
- **founder question:** does she want a rice line in a room whose starch argument
  is masa? It is honest to the comida's structure and it slightly blurs the
  cleanest sentence in the border. Recorded rather than resolved.

### Chiles pasilla oaxaqueño, stuffed and battered
- **course:** main
- **tier:** regional — Oaxacan. The chile is the state's own and the filling is
  documented as the valley's usual one.
- **season:** none
- **making:** the smoked pasilla oaxaqueño stuffed with shredded beef or pork
  cooked with garlic, tomato, onion, raisins, olives and capers; battered and
  fried.
- **why this room:** it is the smoked chile doing the work the meat usually does,
  and it is the dish that proves this kitchen's heat is an ingredient and not a
  headline — a smoked chile is a flavour before it is a temperature.
- **why not Acapulco:** a stuffed, battered, fried chile served hot.
- **period:** secure; the chile is endemic to the region and the stuffing
  technique is colonial.
- **founder question:** none.

### Chiles de agua, stuffed with quesillo
- **course:** main
- **tier:** signature. The chile de agua is **endemic to the Valles Centrales** —
  that is the sources' word — and the cheese is the valley's own. Two valley
  exclusives in one object; nothing else in the catalogue can hold it.
- **season:** none as written, though sources say the chile is at its best and
  hottest in the dry weeks before the rains, when it goes unirrigated.
- **making:** the chile roasted and peeled, stuffed with quesillo or with beans
  and quesillo, and either battered or not.
- **why this room:** it is the room's two most local ingredients put inside each
  other, and it is the only dish here that would be impossible one valley over.
- **why not Acapulco:** a hot chile with melted cheese in it.
- **period:** secure.
- **founder question:** the season note above is a quality claim rather than an
  availability claim, so I have left `season: none`. Does she want the dry-season
  note on the line anyway?

## Desserts

*Six, and the reason for six is in the opening section: this room's ending is
already written and it is coffee and chairs against a wall.*

### Nicuatole, set in a clay dish
- **course:** dessert
- **tier:** signature. Sources place it in the Valles Centrales — a Zapotec town
  in the valley is named as its home — and call it the oldest dessert in Oaxaca.
  It is not made outside the state.
- **season:** none
- **making:** four things: corn, water, cinnamon, and piloncillo or sugar. Cooked
  down and left to set in clay dishes until it is firm enough to cut. Made
  entirely ahead and it does not care how long the afternoon runs.
- **why this room:** it is a dessert made of corn, which means the meal begins
  and ends in the same material. The recipe is described as passed almost
  exclusively woman to woman and never written, which is her premise's sentence
  about the mole said again about the sweet: *nobody has ever written it down.*
- **why not Acapulco:** it is masa, set, at room temperature, in a clay dish, in
  a room with a metate in it. Acapulco's sweets are frozen, coconut, or bought.
- **period:** pre-Hispanic in origin; sugar and cinnamon are the colonial
  additions and maguey honey the older sweetener. Entirely secure for 1954.
- **founder question:** none. This is the single best dessert find in the file.

### Pan de yema, for dunking
- **course:** dessert
- **tier:** signature. Sources give its home as a valley village and it is the
  bread the valley is known for.
- **season:** none, and the flag is worth carrying: it is strongly associated
  with November and Day of the Dead, and it is also explicitly named as the
  wedding and baptism bread and as the thing kept in the house for any
  celebration. So the association is not a restriction.
- **making:** wheat flour, butter, sugar, anise and salt with a great many egg
  yolks and no liquid at all — the yolks ARE the liquid — kneaded by hand in
  wooden troughs and baked in an adobe or brick oven. Bought from a village
  baker in practice; a host makes it or gets it.
- **why this room:** it is bread made to be dunked, and the thing it is dunked in
  is the drink this room ends on. And the Zapotec custom attached to it is
  visiting neighbours and godparents and being given a piece at the door, which
  is her reply convention in food: *nobody is invited; the door is open.*
- **why not Acapulco:** a rich egg bread eaten warm with a hot drink, inland, in
  the cool at the end of the day.
- **period:** secure; the bread and the custom both long predate the room.
- **founder question:** the drink it goes with — chocolate de agua — is a DRINK
  and this room has none. Does she want this line held until the drinks pass, so
  the pair arrives together? I have written it as a dish because the bread stands
  on its own and because holding it would leave the desserts at five.

### Buñuelos, and the plate goes on the ground after
- **course:** dessert
- **tier:** regional — Oaxacan. Argued: the buñuelo is Spanish-descended and
  national; three recipes for it are in Sor Juana's book and eighteenth-century
  Mexican manuscripts carry the Christmas-Eve version. What is Oaxacan is the
  clay plate thrown down afterwards, and a tier cannot be claimed on a gesture,
  so the dish is `regional` and the gesture is recorded below.
- **season:** **December, and it binds.** This is the dessert of the valley's
  December feast weeks and it is not made in August.
- **making:** fried, sugared, and made in quantity, served on a cheap clay plate.
- **why this room:** the plate. You eat the buñuelo, you make a wish, you throw
  the clay plate on the ground, and the year's old business goes with it. Sources
  trace the custom to the colonial period and offer two older explanations for
  it, one Mexica and one from a cholera epidemic. It is the loudest thing this
  quiet room does and it is not performed AT anybody.
- **why not Acapulco:** a fried, hot, sugared thing made in a kitchen in
  December, ending in a broken plate. Acapulco's ending is a bucket of ices and
  nobody bothering.
- **period:** secure. The custom is colonial and the recipes are in
  eighteenth-century Mexican manuscripts.
- **founder question:** **two, and both matter.** First: `season: December` in a
  room booked in a backyard in August is the mango problem from the other end —
  Acapulco's mango was summer against a December premise, and this is December
  against an August booking. Second and larger: **is the smashed plate a dish
  line at all, or is it a `bank_kind = 'host_act'`?** Acapulco's file hit this
  exact shape with its lunch-put-back-out line and the answer there was that a
  mechanism does not go in the dish pool. The difference here is that this one
  NAMES A FOOD and has a making level, which that one did not — so my reading is
  that the buñuelo is a dish and the plate is a host act, and they should be
  written twice, in two tables. Hers to route.

### Nieve de leche quemada
- **course:** dessert
- **tier:** regional — Oaxacan. Argued: burnt-milk sweets exist elsewhere, so
  `signature` overclaims; Larousse lists *nieve de leche quemada* in its
  Oaxaca-exclusive sweets and it is the city's classic flavour, so `repertoire`
  would let it wander somewhere it is not from.
- **season:** none
- **making:** milk cooked until it browns, then frozen. Made ahead by definition,
  or bought from a valley nevero.
- **why this room:** it is the cold thing in a room with nothing else cold in it,
  and it is taken rather than served.
- **why not Acapulco:** that room already owns the frozen category outright —
  coconut ice is its anchor line and a bucket of paletas is its delivery
  mechanism. **This is the closest the two files come to colliding and it is
  worth saying so plainly.** They stay separate on material and on register:
  Acapulco's is coconut, coastal, wet, eaten standing up in a swimsuit;
  this is burnt milk, valley, eaten sitting down at the end of a comida. If she
  reads that as too close, this is the line to cut.
- **period:** the craft is claimed at over a hundred years and six generations by
  the local press and is not archivally pinned — see the flags. What IS pinned is
  that the garden everyone associates with it was only organised as a nieve
  market in 1980, so the place must never appear in the line.
- **founder question:** does the Acapulco proximity above worry her? I have kept
  it because the material and the register differ, and because dropping it would
  leave this room with no cold dish at all, which is its own kind of wrong.

### Nieve de jiotilla
- **course:** dessert
- **tier:** signature. Larousse lists it among sweets exclusive to Oaxaca, and
  the jiotilla is a cactus fruit of the region. No other room could name it.
- **season:** **probably summer, and I could not confirm it.** The fruit is
  seasonal and I did not find a source stating its months. Flagged rather than
  guessed — rule 3.
- **making:** the fruit and ice. Made ahead or bought.
- **why this room:** it is a wild fruit from the dry hills turned into something
  cold, which is the only luxury this house permits itself, and it exists for a
  few weeks.
- **why not Acapulco:** it is a desert cactus fruit from a high valley. There is
  no version of this that is coastal.
- **period:** no flag on the fruit, which is native and gathered; the same
  unpinned dating as the nieve craft above.
- **founder question:** the season. Does she want the line with `season` blank
  until somebody confirms the months, or held until then? My reading: blank and
  flagged beats a guessed month, and the note travels in this file.

### Calabaza en dulce, with figs in the syrup
- **course:** dessert
- **tier:** regional — southern Mexican. Argued honestly: this is made in Oaxaca,
  Puebla and the Valley of Mexico and is one of the country's principal Day of
  the Dead sweets, so `signature` is false. The Oaxacan version is distinguished
  by yellow squash and by figs going into the syrup, which is what the line
  names.
- **season:** **autumn, and it binds.** It is the All Saints sweet — one of its
  Mexican names is literally the All Saints squash — and it belongs to a season.
- **making:** squash cooked in piloncillo syrup with cinnamon, clove, pepper,
  star anise and orange, until the liquid thickens. Made ahead, keeps.
- **why this room:** it is a sweet made for the dead to smell, put out for people
  who are alive. Her house's whole tense is *since before*, and this is the dish
  where the people who are since-before are actually expected.
- **why not Acapulco:** a hot-made, syrup-heavy, spiced squash preserve eaten at
  room temperature in November. Every word of that is the wrong room.
- **period:** secure.
- **founder question:** three of the six desserts now carry a season — December,
  autumn, and an unconfirmed summer. Is a seasonal dessert shelf what she wants
  for a room whose premise is an undated Sunday, or should the pool carry more
  year-round sweets and fewer feast ones? This is a shape question about the
  room, not about any one line.

## Held, not cut — evidence exists and is not enough yet

Recorded so a later pass does not re-derive them from scratch, and so nobody
reads their absence as a ruling (rule 29).

- **Marquesote, ante, empanadas de Corpus, conserva de tejocote.** All four are
  named as Oaxacan sweets in Larousse's Oaxaca entry, which is real positive
  evidence. None of them appears in that same entry's separate Valles Centrales
  list, and I found no valley attribution or period detail for any of them. They
  are probably fine and I could not show it.
- **The Valles Centrales breads Larousse DOES name** — molletes, muertitos, pan
  amarillo, pan de manteca, pelonas, tarazones, roscas de yema — where the valley
  attribution is explicit and I have no description of any of them to write a
  line from. This is the most promising unworked seam in the room.
- **Gusanos de maguey as a dish** rather than as the salt. Season unknown.
- **Frijoles con hierba de conejo**, named under Oaxaca by Larousse; folded into
  the avocado-leaf bean line's note rather than written twice.
- **Piedrazos, tesupos**, both named among the Oaxacan antojitos and both of
  which I could not describe from any source I reached.
