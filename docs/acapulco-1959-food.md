## What is here, and what is not

**Appetizers: 8. Mains: 7. Desserts: 7.** Twenty-two lines.

**The mains list is short on purpose and will not honestly grow much past
seven.** Rule 25.3 is the binding constraint here more than in any other room
in the library: this voice says outright that there is no staff and that
things appear rather than being served, so the room gets exactly one fire and
everything else has to be cold or have been finished hours ago. A room that
allows one fire does not have fifteen mains. Padding it would mean either a
second cook working through the evening or dishes that go out hot to order,
and both of those are a restaurant wearing the room's clothes. The appetizers
and desserts carry the count because cold things and made-ahead sweets are
what this structure actually produces.

### Cut, and why

**Pozole verde de Guerrero.** The hardest cut in the file and the one most
likely to be overruled. It is Guerrero's most identifying dish, it is
everyday rather than festival food in Acapulco, and the state's own tourism
and food press call it the most representative dish of the port. Cut on three
grounds that stack. First, it is a pot that has been going for hours, which is
Oaxaca's entire structure — mole since yesterday, somebody sent to stir — and
two Mexican rooms whose centre of gravity is a simmering pot have converged on
the axis rule 26 says to keep disjoint. Second, it is nixtamalized corn, and
masa is the wall (below). Third, its identity is a named weekday — *jueves
pozolero* — and this room cannot pronounce a schedule. Founder's to overrule;
if she wants it, it should come in as Acapulco's and be removed from anything
Oaxaca might later claim.

**Pescado a la talla.** Acapulco's most famous dish by a wide margin, and it
fails on period. The dish is attributed to Beto Godoy and Gregoria Galeana at
Barra Vieja, and the accounts date it to a little more than half a century
before they were written — so roughly the 1960s or 1970s, after this room. The
plain whole fish split over coals, which is what they were doing before the
chile rub became the dish, stands in for it and is in the mains below.

**Aguachile.** Two failures at once. It is Sinaloan, not Guerrero, so rule 6
refuses it before period does; and the raw-shrimp-in-lime form we mean by the
word consolidated in the 1970s with Sinaloa's commercial shrimp boom. The
older aguachile was a mountain dish of dried meat softened in chiltepín water.
Neither version belongs on this table in 1959.

**Tiritas de pescado.** Zihuatanejo, up the Costa Grande, and the local
account dates the dish to about fifty years ago — the 1970s. It also arrives
one adjective away from Amalfi's authored line, "the fish, sliced thin, lemon
on it", which is a second reason to leave it alone.

**Shark tamales.** Cut against positive evidence, which is worth recording.
There is real 1950s Acapulco hotel material naming shark tamales as a dish a
place was known for, so period is not the problem. Masa is. Tamales are one of
the four words the Oaxaca voice names as its table words, and a tamal on this
terrace hands Acapulco the other room's noun for nothing.

**Guacamole.** Legal — it is repertoire, and rule 6 lets repertoire go
anywhere the register fits — and cut anyway. It would sit identically well on
both tables, so it buys the plate no distinction at all, and the whole reason
these two rooms need separate food is the measure in rule 26.

**Huachinango a la veracruzana** and **vuelve a la vida.** Both Gulf. Rule 6
says `regional` travels within its cuisine and not its country; Veracruz is
not the Guerrero coast any more than Ligurian is Campanian. The plain shrimp
cocktail below does the work vuelve a la vida was wanted for, without the
Veracruz name on it.

### What could not be dated confidently

- **The ceviche.** The Acapulco-style ceviche recipe I read is from Josefina
  Velázquez de León's bilingual regional cookbook. The copy digitised at UTSA
  is the **1969** printing. The title was **first published in 1947** and ran
  to eight editions through 1971, but I could not confirm the Acapulco recipe
  was in the 1947 setting rather than added later. So: the dish is documented
  in a Mexican regional cookbook within a decade of this room and by an author
  collecting recipes from the 1930s onward, and the exact date is open. Flagged
  in the entry's founder question.
- **Cold octopus with lime at Acapulco specifically in 1959.** Octopus is a
  Pacific-coast staple and the preparation is trivially old, but I found no
  source tying it to this port in this decade. Positive evidence is thinner
  here than anywhere else in the file.
- **Fruit with chile and salt.** Salt, lime and chile piquín on cut fruit is
  old. The commercial chile-lime powders are not — the best-known one dates to
  1985. Any line here has to say salt and chile and not name a powder.
- **Mango season.** Mango in Mexico is a spring-and-summer fruit and this room
  is December. Marked seasonal below, with the tension noted: rule 25.1 books
  the room in a backyard in August, where mango is right and December is not.

## The border, stated once

Every line below is checked against it, so it is worth saying in one place.

Oaxaca's food is **hot and made of corn**. The mole started days ago, the
tortillas came off a comal this morning, the tamales are in the steamer, and
the stove's only job tonight is keeping it warm. The kitchen is the speaker
and it has authority. The plate is *presented*.

Acapulco's food is **cold, or it went on a fire, and it is made of lime**.
Nothing here was cooked by anybody who is still cooking. There is no pot, no
comal and no stove in this voice, because there is no kitchen in this voice —
things appear and nobody serves them. What seasons a dish is lime and salt,
and the founder's own authored line already says so: more lime than anybody
needs.

**So: heat and masa on one side, cold and lime on the other.** That wall cuts
the pot dishes, the masa dishes and anything that has to be finished while
people are sitting down. It is also why the room reads to that kitchen as
slightly scandalous, which is her word for the border.

## Appetizers

### Oysters on ice, and more lime than anybody needs
- **course:** appetizer
- **tier:** repertoire — and the honest note is that the *line* is signature
  and the *dish* is not. Oysters on ice already sit in Westhampton's list, so
  tiering this `signature` would claim an exclusive the catalogue has already
  spent. What is Acapulco's here is the lime and the quantity of it.
- **season:** none
- **making:** none. Ice, a tray, a knife, and the limes cut before anybody
  arrives. Survives the reset because it is already on ice.
- **why this room:** it is the only food in the room that requires being told
  nothing — you take one from the ice while somebody is still talking to you.
- **why not Oaxaca:** raw, cold, and it needs no kitchen; that kitchen's whole
  authority is that it started days ago.
- **period:** oysters off this coast are a plain coastal fact, and the Costa
  Chica beds are still the local reference for them.
- **founder question:** none. This is her line, quoted from the exemplars.

### Ceviche, in a glass, with saltines
- **course:** appetizer
- **tier:** signature. It carries the city's name and it should never travel.
  The argument: this is the one dish in the file whose regional identity *is*
  this room, and letting it move would erase the distinction the matrix is
  defending between the two Mexican rooms.
- **season:** none
- **making:** all of it before anybody comes. Fish cut small, three hours in
  lime, then tomato, onion, pickled chile, oregano, avocado at the end. It
  holds cold on ice for the length of an afternoon and gets no worse.
- **why this room:** it is served in whatever glass is free, with crackers
  out of a box, and eaten standing — the room's elegance carried by objects
  doing casual duty, which is exactly what the lexicon says.
- **why not Oaxaca:** lime does the cooking. No fire, no pot, no masa, and
  nobody had to be up early.
- **period:** documented as an Acapulco-style recipe in Josefina Velázquez de
  León's bilingual regional cookbook — fish in small cubes, three hours in
  lime, served ice cold in glasses, with corn chips or saltine crackers. The
  saltine is in the source, not invented for the register.
- **founder question:** the copy I read is the 1969 printing of a book first
  published in 1947. Does she want the line held until the 1947 setting is
  checked, or is a documented Mexican regional recipe within a decade of the
  room close enough?

### Coctel de camarón, cold, with a spoon standing in it
- **course:** appetizer
- **tier:** repertoire. Argued: the shellfish cocktail is a Mexican coastal
  standard rather than one coast's property, and the versions with strong
  regional claims are Gulf ones. Repertoire is the honest tier and it keeps
  the Veracruz name off the table.
- **season:** none
- **making:** shrimp cooked and chilled in the morning, everything else cut
  cold, assembled into glasses whenever somebody wants one. Holds on ice.
- **why this room:** it is a drink and a plate at once, it is held in one
  hand, and it can be picked up an hour after it was put down.
- **why not Oaxaca:** cold shellfish in a glass. There is no glass of cold
  seafood anywhere in a kitchen whose centre is a pot.
- **period:** the Mexican shellfish cocktail is an early-twentieth-century
  coastal-marisquería form and is well established by 1959.
- **founder question:** she may want the fuller version by name. I have left
  the Veracruz name off deliberately — see the cuts.

### Pulpo, cooked yesterday, cold, lime and oil
- **course:** appetizer
- **tier:** regional — Mexican Pacific coast. Argued: octopus is a coastal
  preparation, not a Guerrero-only one, so `signature` would overclaim; but it
  does not travel inland, so `repertoire` would let it go somewhere it has no
  business.
- **season:** none
- **making:** entirely yesterday. Cooked, cooled, cut, dressed. It improves
  sitting in the lime and is the single best dish in the file for a table that
  keeps being relaid, because the longer it waits the better it is.
- **why this room:** it is the dish that does not care when dinner starts,
  which is the only arrangement this room makes.
- **why not Oaxaca:** cold, from the water, and nothing about it was warm at
  any point tonight.
- **period:** the preparation is old and unremarkable. What I could not find
  is a source putting it in Acapulco in this decade specifically — the
  thinnest evidence in the file.
- **founder question:** does she want it on the thinner evidence, or held?

### Camarones al mojo de ajo, in the pan they were cooked in
- **course:** appetizer
- **tier:** regional — Mexican Pacific coast.
- **season:** none
- **making:** the one hot appetizer, and it is four minutes in one pan. The
  pan goes on the table and nobody transfers it to anything. This passes rule
  25.3 only because it is one pan handed over, not a plate composed for a
  person.
- **why this room:** garlic on your hands for the rest of the night, and
  nobody offered it to you — the pan was put down and you reached.
- **why not Oaxaca:** it is cooked in the moment and eaten in the moment,
  which is the exact opposite of a kitchen whose boast is about elapsed time.
- **period:** garlic shrimp is a long-standing coastal Mexican preparation and
  raises no dating flag.
- **founder question:** this is the only line in the appetizers that needs
  heat at the moment. Does the room allow it, or is the fire reserved for the
  fish?

### Fish fried yesterday and left in vinegar and onions. Eaten cold
- **course:** appetizer
- **tier:** regional — coastal Mexican, by way of Spain.
- **season:** none
- **making:** yesterday, whole. It is designed to be eaten cold days later —
  the preservation *is* the recipe — so it survives every reset the table has
  in it.
- **why this room:** this is the premise expressed as food. Lunch does not end
  because the food was never keeping time in the first place.
- **why not Oaxaca:** cold fish out of vinegar has nothing to do with a stove
  that is being kept warm, and there is no masa near it.
- **period:** escabeche is centuries old on both sides of the Atlantic and is
  entirely secure for 1959.
- **founder question:** the name at the table. "Escabeche" is a table word;
  the plain English description above may be closer to her register. Her call.

### Jícama, cucumber and green fruit, cut cold, salt and chile and lime
- **course:** appetizer
- **tier:** repertoire.
- **season:** none as written; if green mango is used, summer.
- **making:** cut cold in the afternoon, put on a tray, left where people
  walk past. Nobody assembles anything.
- **why this room:** wet hands, salt already on everybody, and it is the only
  thing on the table you can eat while standing in a doorway.
- **why not Oaxaca:** it is raw and it is cold and it took nine minutes. That
  kitchen's food is an argument about time and this is a tray of cut fruit.
- **period:** salt, lime and chile piquín on cut fruit is old. The commercial
  chile-lime powders are not — the best-known dates to 1985 — so the line must
  say salt and chile and never name a powder.
- **founder question:** none.

### Peanuts fried with garlic and too much chile
- **course:** appetizer
- **tier:** repertoire.
- **season:** none
- **making:** days ahead if you like. They sit in a bowl and get taken from
  all night; the bowl is refilled by whoever notices, which is nobody serving
  anybody.
- **why this room:** it is the food that is still there at the hour when
  nobody is checking, and it goes with a drink somebody handed you.
- **why not Oaxaca:** nothing about it is presented, and that kitchen presents
  its food as the one thing it presents.
- **period:** unremarkable and secure.
- **founder question:** none.

## Mains

### Fish off the grill, whole, eaten with your hands and no ceremony
- **course:** main
- **tier:** signature. This is the room's main and should not travel.
- **season:** none
- **making:** the one fire. Split, salted, on the coals, off the coals, onto
  a board. Nobody portions it and nobody plates it.
- **why this room:** it is the only dish that requires the evening to arrive
  at it, and it still does not require an hour — it goes on when somebody
  decides it goes on.
- **why not Oaxaca:** it is fire and it is fast, in a room whose neighbour's
  whole claim is a stove that has been on since yesterday.
- **period:** whole fish over coals on this coast is not datable because it
  never began. Worth recording: the chile-rubbed named version, pescado a la
  talla, is attributed to Barra Vieja in roughly the 1960s or 1970s, so this
  room gets the fish and not the name.
- **founder question:** none. This is her line, quoted from the exemplars.

### Morisqueta — white rice, beans from the pot, whatever came out of the water on top
- **course:** main
- **tier:** regional — Guerrero coast. Argued at length because it is the
  strongest border line in the file: this is Costa Grande and Costa Chica food,
  and the rice itself came through this port on the Manila galleons. It is
  regional to the Guerrero coast and to the Michoacán tierra caliente next
  door, and nowhere else.
- **season:** none
- **making:** rice in the afternoon, beans before that, both of them holding
  in their pots. Somebody puts fish or shrimp on top when they come back from
  the water. It feeds whoever wanders in at whatever hour they wander in.
- **why this room:** dinner starts when the last of you wanders back, and this
  is what that sentence looks like as a dish — nothing spoils by waiting.
- **why not Oaxaca:** the starch is the whole argument. That kitchen's starch
  is masa, made this morning, warm, in a basket. This room's starch is white
  rice that arrived by sea through this port. Two different countries of the
  plate, one border apart.
- **period:** the dish's Manila galleon lineage runs through Acapulco itself
  and its presence on the Guerrero coast long predates the room.
- **founder question:** the name. "Morisqueta" is a table word on that coast
  and a guest there would say it; a guest in Brooklyn would not. Does rule
  25.2 admit it on the strength of the first, or does the line become "rice,
  beans, and whatever came out of the water"?

### Shrimp with garlic, in more butter than is sensible
- **course:** main
- **tier:** regional — Mexican Pacific coast.
- **season:** none
- **making:** one pan on the same fire, after the fish. Poured into a dish
  and put down.
- **why this room:** it is the overstatement said as logistics. There was
  enough butter, and then there was more.
- **why not Oaxaca:** butter and a pan, at speed. There is no butter in that
  kitchen's argument and nothing there is at speed.
- **period:** secure.
- **founder question:** this and the appetizer version are the same technique
  at two sizes. She may want one, not both.

### Chicken on the same coals, rubbed with chile and lime, after the fish
- **course:** main
- **tier:** repertoire.
- **season:** none
- **making:** the same fire, already lit, doing a second job. Rubbed hours
  before; it can sit rubbed all afternoon and only improves.
- **why this room:** it is the answer to the people who came back late, and
  it costs nobody a second piece of equipment.
- **why not Oaxaca:** grilled and eaten with hands. That kitchen's chicken
  would be under something that took two days.
- **period:** secure, and deliberately unnamed — the named chile-rub dishes of
  this coast date later.
- **founder question:** none.

### Langosta, split, on the fire, and lime
- **course:** main
- **tier:** regional — Mexican Pacific coast.
- **season:** none
- **making:** split ahead, on the coals for minutes, onto the same board the
  fish came off. No sauce, because a sauce is a person standing over a stove.
- **why this room:** the good glasses anyway, said in food. Something expensive
  handled carelessly and eaten with hands on a wall.
- **why not Oaxaca:** it is the sea, and it is offhand about being the sea.
  That room has no register for offhand luxury; its luxury is elapsed time.
- **period:** Pacific lobster and this decade's resort coast are a documented
  pairing, and the preparation is the simplest one available.
- **founder question:** does this read as the room, or as the hotel the room
  is standing next to? It is the one line in the file where the 1959 resort
  era could pull the voice toward a place instead of a table.

### Crab, cracked, in a pile, with a bowl for the shells
- **course:** main
- **tier:** regional — Mexican Pacific coast.
- **season:** none
- **making:** boiled and cooled in the morning, cracked whenever. Entirely
  cold, entirely made ahead, and the pile grows all evening.
- **why this room:** the shells are the clock. Time is told by what has
  happened, and this is what has happened.
- **why not Oaxaca:** shellfish eaten with hands, cold, with nothing cooked
  around it.
- **period:** secure.
- **founder question:** none.

### Whatever lunch left, put back out cold when dinner starts
- **course:** main
- **tier:** signature. It is not a recipe; it is the gesture as food.
- **season:** none
- **making:** none, twice. It was already made once.
- **why this room:** it is the premise stated as a dish — lunch never exactly
  ends, and the table gets reset around whoever refuses to leave it. New cloth,
  new candles, the same food, colder.
- **why not Oaxaca:** that room's food is kept warm, deliberately, by a stove
  whose only job that is. This one is kept cold by nobody bothering.
- **period:** not a period claim.
- **founder question:** this is the one line I am least sure of and it is the
  one closest to her premise. Is it a dish the pool can hold, or is it really
  a host act — the reset — wearing a dish's clothes? If it is the second it
  belongs in `bank_kind = 'host_act'` and not here.

## Desserts

### Coconut ice, eaten wet, standing up
- **course:** dessert
- **tier:** signature. Hers, and the whole file's anchor.
- **season:** none
- **making:** days ahead, in the freezer, taken by whoever wants one.
- **why this room:** you are still wet and you did not sit down, and nobody
  handed it to you.
- **why not Oaxaca:** frozen. Nothing in that kitchen is cold on purpose
  except the mezcal, and that is poured, not taken.
- **period:** frozen sweets in Mexico long predate this room, and Guerrero is
  the country's principal coconut state.
- **founder question:** none. Her line, from the exemplars.

### Cocadas, on a tray, going soft in the heat
- **course:** dessert
- **tier:** regional — Guerrero coast. Argued: cocada is made on every hot
  coast in the country — Colima, Oaxaca's own coast, Veracruz — so it is not
  signature;
  but Guerrero is the main producer and trays of them are the port's own image,
  so it is not free-travelling repertoire either.
- **season:** none
- **making:** days ahead. Keeps at room temperature indefinitely, needs no
  plate, no fork and no cold, and does not notice the table being relaid.
- **why this room:** it is on a tray on the wall next to the good glasses and
  it has been there since before you arrived.
- **why not Oaxaca:** it is coconut, from the coast, and the sweet in that
  kitchen's evening is not a sweet — it is café de olla and the chairs going
  against the wall.
- **period:** cocada arrives in New Spain through the Manila galleon trade
  into this port and is entirely secure for 1959.
- **founder question:** Oaxaca has a coast, and cocadas are made on it. If she
  ever writes food for Oaxaca's coast rather than its valley, this line is the
  first collision. Worth her knowing now.

### Paletas in a bucket of ice. Take one
- **course:** dessert
- **tier:** repertoire. Argued: the paletería is Michoacán by origin, and by
  the 1950s it had spread nationally through Tocumbo migration — so it is not
  a regional claim by then, it is everywhere, which is what repertoire means.
- **season:** none
- **making:** bought. That is the entire entry.
- **why this room:** nobody serves them and nobody offers. There is a bucket
  and there is you, and the mechanism the room describes — things appear — is
  literally the delivery method.
- **why not Oaxaca:** a bucket of bought ices is the opposite of a kitchen
  that makes its claim about time. That room would be insulted, which is the
  border working.
- **period:** the paleta business begins in Tocumbo in the 1930s and the first
  shop in the capital opens in 1946; by 1959 it is a national fixture.
- **founder question:** does a bought thing belong in a room this committed to
  the good glasses? I think yes — the room's whole trick is expensive objects
  used carelessly and cheap ones used without apology — but it is hers.

### Flan, cold, cut badly
- **course:** dessert
- **tier:** repertoire, and the honest note is that it does no border work at
  all. It would sit as comfortably in Oaxaca as here, and by the test in the
  brief that means it is wrong for one of the two rooms. My reading: it is
  wrong for **Oaxaca**, because that room's ending is already written — café
  de olla, chairs against the wall, and no dessert course at all — so flan has
  nowhere to land there. It stays here by default rather than by argument, and
  that is worth saying out loud rather than dressing up.
- **season:** none
- **making:** yesterday, in the refrigerator, turned out whenever.
- **why this room:** it is cold, it was made before, and nobody cuts it
  neatly at this hour.
- **why not Oaxaca:** see the tier note. This one is by elimination, not by
  character.
- **period:** secure.
- **founder question:** keep a line that earns its place only by default, or
  cut it and run six desserts?
